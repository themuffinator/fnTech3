# Ghoul2 compressed poses: what fourteen bytes hold

> October 2 correction: direct queries of the retail dedicated engine now
> contradict this record's inference that the existing rest fallback and
> independent bone composition establish correct server posing. All 18
> compared matrices differ, and stored angle overrides do not affect the
> engine's result. The encoded-field observations remain historical evidence;
> hierarchy, inherited animation and coordinate/scale handling remain open.
> See the [runtime comparison](2026-10-02-jka-compatibility.md) and its
> [machine-readable evidence](2026-10-02-jka-compatibility.json).

*2026-09-22. Measured from the shipped skeletons of legitimate retail Jedi
Academy and Jedi Outcast installations. Both installations were read, never
written, and no asset was copied into this repository. The corpus is the
`.gla` files inside `base/assets1.pk3` (Academy, 66 skeletons) and
`base/assets0.pk3` (Outcast, 21 skeletons); the numbers below come from nine
of each, including the full pose pool of both titles' `_humanoid.gla`.*

A `.gla` skeleton holds its bones, a block of per-frame indices, and a pool of
compressed poses those indices reach into. This engine's reader has always
bounds-checked that pool without decoding it. This is what decoding it would
mean, how much of it is now known, and what is still missing.

## The pool stride is fourteen bytes

Taken from the format reference, `CompressedBoneBytes` was 14. It now has its
own evidence, which is worth having because 7 divides these pools too.

For each skeleton, the largest index any frame actually uses, plus one, times
fourteen, equals the pool size **exactly**:

| skeleton | bones | frames | top index | pool | (top+1)x14 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Academy `items/psgun` | 9 | 11 | 30 | 434 | 434 |
| Academy `map_objects/hoth/model` | 7 | 21 | 66 | 938 | 938 |
| Academy `players/_humanoid` | 53 | 21,376 | 599,569 | 8,393,980 | 8,393,980 |
| Outcast `items/psgun` | 9 | 11 | 30 | 434 | 434 |
| Outcast `players/atst` | 23 | 272 | 2,857 | 40,012 | 40,012 |
| Outcast `players/_humanoid` | 72 | 17,278 | 445,609 | 6,238,540 | 6,238,540 |

Eighteen of eighteen, both titles, no remainder. A stride of seven would fit as
arithmetic but would leave the upper half of every pool unreachable by any
frame, which no shipped file would do. Fourteen it is.

The frame block confirms itself the same way: its indices are three bytes
each, and the block is `bones x frames x 3` rounded up to a multiple of four
(the two humanoids need no rounding; both are already a multiple).
The nine-bone, eleven-frame `psgun` spends 300 bytes on 297 bytes of index;
the seven-bone, twenty-one-frame `hoth` spends 444 on 441.

## The first four words are a rotation

**Observed.** Read the fourteen bytes as seven little-endian sixteen-bit
words. Subtract 32,768 from the first four and take their length. Across
Academy's entire humanoid pool -- 599,570 distinct poses -- the mean is
**16,380.97**, and across Outcast's 445,610 it is **16,382.40**. Over the whole
of both pools -- not a sample of them -- the length stays between 0.998 and
1.003, varying by 0.382% end to end in Academy and 0.362% in Outcast.

> **Corrected below.** The engine's own constants are a mid-point of 32,766
> and a divisor of 16,383, not 32,768 and 16,384. The reasoning in this
> section stands; the last two digits of its answer do not. See *The engine's
> own constants, and a correction*.

**Inferred.** Nothing but a unit quaternion is that constant. The scale is
therefore `1/16384`, which puts both titles' mean length within two parts in
ten thousand of one. 16,384 is also the only power of two in the running,
which is what a fixed-point encoder would pick.

The first word is the scalar. In a rotation of nothing it sits near the top of
the scale while the other three sit at the bias point: the commonest value of
word 0 across Academy's pool is 49,148 (about `+1.0`) and of words 1 to 3 is
32,766 (about `0`). The two-to-three count of slack either side is the
animation data's own, not the encoding's.

No other four-word window behaves this way. Windows 1-4, 2-5 and 3-6 spread by
15% to 25%, and reading the same four as plain signed words spreads by 18%.

## The last three words are a deviation from the rest pose

**Observed.** They are biased about 32,768 like the rotation.

The decisive witness is a model that does not move. Academy's
`map_objects/imp_mine/turret_cannon.gla` has four bones, three identical
frames, and real geometry: `Bone_body` sits 6.68 units from its parent and
`Bone_barrel` 28.86 units from its. Every pose translation in it is **zero** --
0 or -1 in every component of every pose of every frame. A number that is zero
while the bone it describes is twenty-nine units from its parent cannot be that
bone's position, and cannot be its offset from its parent either. It can only
be how far the bone has moved from where it was modelled, which in a model
that never moves is nowhere.

Academy's `items/psgun.gla` says the same from the other side. Its
`model_root` is zero, and its three foot bones carry the *same non-zero* value
in all eleven frames: a fixed displacement from the modelled pose, held
steady, which is a sensible thing for a deviation to be and a strange thing
for a position to be.

This reading was written off once here, on a bad test. Exact zeros are rare in
the humanoid pool -- 438 of 599,570 -- which was taken to mean the deviation
is rarely nothing. It means only that a hand-animated skeleton rarely leaves a
bone *exactly* where it was modelled. A static prop does, exactly, and that is
the case that settles it.

## The scale of that deviation is 1/64

Two independent lines of evidence say so, and the one thing that looked like
it said otherwise turned out to be a mistake on my part.

First: `model_root` carries exactly `(0, 0, -1536)` -- on the z axis, and the
same number -- in both titles' `_humanoid.gla` and in Academy's unrelated
`map_objects/hoth/model.gla`, in almost every frame of all three. At `1/64`
that is -24.00 units on the nose. Root travel is the right order too: the
`Motion` bone peaks at 8,731 in Outcast's humanoid and 6,799 in Academy's,
which at `1/64` are 136 and 106 units, about right for a leap or a roll.

Second, and strongest: every skeleton's root deviation is a whole number of units
at `1/64`. Across all 87 skeletons of both titles, `model_root` in frame 0
deviates on the **z axis only** -- x and y are zero in every single one -- and
takes just seven values:

| raw | 0 | -768 | -1216 | -1536 | -4096 | -7680 | -9856 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| at `1/64` | 0 | -12 | -19 | -24 | -64 | -120 | -154 |
| skeletons | 16 | 3 | 2 | 62 | 2 | 1 | 1 |

All six non-zero values are exact multiples of 64. Six values landing on a
multiple of 64 by chance is about one in 10^11. These read like a drop from
the model's origin to the ground, authored in whole units, which is what a
`1/64` fixed point would store them as.

That quantisation is specific to the root and does not generalise, which is
consistent rather than contradictory: across the humanoid pools only 3.1% of
deviation components are multiples of 64 against a 1.56% baseline, and
hand-animated values have no reason to land on whole units.

Against it, at first: at `1/64` the typical bone deviation looked far too
large to be one. The median across Outcast's 72 humanoid bones is 47 units and
Academy's is 11, and 47 units is longer than the character's leg.

That objection was comparing against the wrong thing. Sorting the bones by how
much they move says what these numbers are:

| | Academy | Outcast |
| --- | --- | --- |
| moves most | `rhumerus` 37, `lradius` 32, finger joints ~30 | `rhand` 56, `mc7` 56, finger joints ~56 |
| moves least | `jaw`, `face`, `ceyebrow`, `lblip2`: **0** | the same four: **0** |
| feet | `ltalus` 3.4, `rtalus` 4.8 | `ltarsal` 13.9, `rtarsal` 12.0 |
| head | `cranium` 12.8, `cervical` 8.9 | `cranium` 10.1, `cervical` 5.1 |

Hands and fingers move furthest; the face does not move at all, which is right
for multiplayer, where it never animates. If the deviation were an offset from
the parent bone, a finger joint and a jaw would both be small, because neither
moves much *relative to what it hangs off*. They are 56 and 0. A displacement
that accumulates down the chain -- so that raising an arm carries the hand
fifty units -- is the only reading that produces this shape, and at `1/64`
every one of these figures is what that bone really does: a hand travels about
fifty units when an arm goes up, a head barely moves, a face never does.

The correlation with a bone's rest distance from the origin is weak (0.15 in
Academy, 0.22 in Outcast), which is expected rather than troubling: arms swing
and heads do not, so how far a bone sits from the origin does not determine
how far it travels.

So `1/64` is the scale, on two grounds here -- the root's whole-unit
quantisation and the plausibility of every bone's travel -- and on a third,
independent one in the section after next, where holding the composition rule
fixed and sweeping the scale puts a sharp error floor exactly at `1/64`.

One more thing the scale might involve: the skeleton header carries a scale
of its own, at offset 72, and it is not always one. Across Academy's 66
skeletons it is 0.64 in 46 of them, 1.0 in 15, and 0.576, 0.6, 0.75, 0.8 or
1.44 in one each; across Outcast's 21 it is 1.0 in 17 and 0.5, 0.6 or 0.64 in
the rest. A per-file factor cannot by itself explain the trouble above, which
is a disagreement between the root bone and the limbs *within* one file, but
it is a term that would have to be accounted for and is recorded here so it is
not rediscovered.

The engine's own arithmetic was looked for and not found, and that negative
is weaker than it sounds. Sweeping either binary from the start of `.text`
turned up no multiply of an index by fourteen, no run of seven consecutive
word reads through one pointer, and no load of any candidate scale constant
from `.rdata`. But both engines embed their jump tables *inside* `.text` --
Academy's game-import table sits at `0x45d34c`, right after a function that
ends at `0x45d348` -- so a linear sweep desynchronises on table bytes and
resynchronises somewhere arbitrary. It will miss real instructions, and how
many it missed is not known. These searches found nothing; they did not
establish that there is nothing.
That search can be resumed from the format idents, which sit at `0x4a8f15`
and `0x4a9286` in `jamp.exe` and `0x485146` and `0x4853f1` in `jk2mp.exe`.

Two negatives worth not repeating. Searching for a routine that loads the
header's `ofsCompBonePool` at offset `0x58` alongside `numBones` at `0x54`
turns up one candidate in `jk2mp.exe`, at `0x40297d`; it was read and it is
argument marshalling, pushing a dozen fields of an unrelated structure, and
the matching offsets are coincidence. Everything else the search found is a
stack-frame slot rather than a structure field.

## How a pose composes: the rest matrix, and nothing else

The rule is `W = D . base`. A bone's matrix in a frame is the decoded pose
applied to that bone's own rest matrix, **with no walk up the hierarchy at
all**. The pose already carries where the bone ended up.

That was found by asking what bones cannot do. Bones do not stretch, so the
right rule keeps every bone the same distance from its parent in every frame
and a wrong rule does not. Six candidates were tried against Academy's
humanoid:

| rule | median bone-length error |
| --- | ---: |
| `W = Wp . L . D` | 99.4% |
| `W = Wp . D . L` | 98.9% |
| `W = Wp . L`, then add T | 99.8% |
| `W = base . D` | 108.8% |
| **`W = D . base`** | **0.47%** |
| `W = Wp . (R.L_R, L_T + T)` | 98.9% |

Two hundred times better than anything else, which is not the kind of margin
that needs a tie-break. Reading the quaternion conjugated, or splitting the
rotation from the translation, all land back in the hundreds of percent.

Measured properly -- on Academy's 42 bones that hang off an anatomical parent,
over 404 frames and 16,968 measurements -- the rule holds bone lengths to a
**median error of 0.034 units, about 0.38% of a bone**, with a p90 of 0.75 and
a p99 of 2.4.

The same sweep pins the scale from a second direction. Holding the rule fixed
and varying only what multiplies words 4 to 6:

| scale | 1/16 | 1/32 | **1/64** | 1/128 | 1/256 | drop it |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| median error, units | 86.2 | 26.2 | **0.076** | 6.5 | 12.2 | 19.1 |

A sharp floor at `1/64` and a climb away from it on both sides.

### Two bones and one skeleton that look wrong and are not

`pelvis` and `lower_lumbar` show errors of 17 and 9 units in Academy. Their
parents are `model_root` and `pelvis`, which are the frame the model hangs in
and the hip that drives it -- the two things that *do* move relative to the
body. Their separation is supposed to change.

Outcast's humanoid looks far worse on this measure -- a median of 4 units and
only one frame in two hundred under half a unit -- and that is the measure's
fault, not the rule's. Its hierarchy is flatter than Academy's: fingers hang
off `thoracic` rather than off a hand, and `ltalus` off `pelvis` rather than
off `ltibia`. A finger 26 units from the chest genuinely moves relative to the
chest when an arm bends, so "distance from parent" is not a bone length in
that file and cannot test anything. Under a rule that walks no hierarchy this
makes no difference to the result, which is part of why the rule is credible.

## The engine's own constants, and a correction

Everything above was measured out of the files. The engine's decompressor was
then read directly -- it begins at RVA `0x86d30` of `jamp.exe` -- and it
settles the constants exactly. It divides and then subtracts:

| | engine | measured here first | agrees? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| rotation | `word / 16383 - 2` | `(word - 32768) / 16384` | **no** |
| displacement | `word / 64 - 512` | `(word - 32768) / 64` | yes |

The displacement was right. **The rotation was not**: the mid-point is 32,766
and the divisor 16,383, not 32,768 and 16,384. That is a real error in what
this engine shipped an hour ago, and it is exactly what the data had been
saying -- the mean quaternion length came out at 0.9998 rather than 1, and
that residual was put down to animation noise when it was the reading being
two parts off. With the engine's constants the same measurement gives 1.0000,
and the rigidity error drops from 0.0783 to 0.0749 units.

The two halves of a pose genuinely disagree with each other about where zero
is. That is Raven's, not a misreading, and the code says so where a reader
would otherwise tidy it away.

Reading the engine also confirmed, positively, a set of constants this reader
had only inferred: `and ecx, 0xffffff` then `imul ecx, ecx, 0xe` on the frame
index -- the three-byte index and the fourteen-byte stride, in one
instruction pair -- and `[header+0x4c]`, `[+0x50]`, `[+0x54]`, `[+0x58]` as
the frame count, frame offset, bone count and pool offset, which are the
offsets this reader uses. The skinning path confirms the mesh side the same
way: five-bit bone references, at most four weights, the `0x300` mask on a
weight's top bits, `1/1023` to scale it, and a thirty-two byte vertex.

That the earlier sweep reported "no multiply by fourteen anywhere" was a false
negative, exactly as the note above warns. The instruction is at `0x497a23`.

## Timing: which frame a bone is on

This was the one thing said to be unmeasurable out of the files, and it is
measurable out of the engine. `G2_TimingModel` begins at `0x497290` and does:

    elapsed = (pauseTime ? pauseTime : now) - startTime
    if (elapsed < 0) elapsed = 0
    frame   = startFrame + (elapsed / 50) * animSpeed

Fifty is a float at `0x566098`, so a Ghoul2 frame is fifty milliseconds and a
speed of one runs at twenty frames a second. It writes three answers to three
places the caller hands it: the whole part is the current frame, the frame
after it is what to blend towards, and the fraction between them is the blend
weight. Bit `0x10` of the flags is the loop flag; set, the next frame wraps,
clear, it holds at the last frame. The bone record's fields sit at `+0x34`
flags, `+0x38` start frame, `+0x3c` end frame, `+0x40` start time, `+0x44`
pause time and `+0x48` speed.

Nothing pauses here: neither title's import table has a call that would set a
pause time, so that term is always nothing.

The two frames are then blended **element by element across all twelve matrix
entries**, weights `lerp` and `1 - lerp` -- a matrix lerp, not a quaternion
slerp. Which frame takes which weight is forced by continuity rather than read
off a register name: at a blend weight of zero the answer has to be the
current frame's pose, so the current frame carries `1 - lerp`.

This is implemented in the server-side Ghoul2 store as `PlayheadAt`, with the
constants named and the arithmetic in the engine's own order.

## What this changes

The reader carries `DecodePose`, `PoseMatrix`, `ComposeMatrix`,
`BoneRestMatrix`, `BoneRestMatrixInverse`, `BonePose`, `BoneMatrix`,
`SurfaceAt`, `TriangleCorners` and `VertexAt`; the store carries `PlayheadAt`,
`BoneMatrices` and `BoltMatrix`. **`G_G2_GETBOLT` is served**, with
`G_G2_GETBOLT_NOREC` and `G_G2_GETBOLT_NOREC_NOROT` beside it -- retail
separates those three only by how much of a skeleton it is willing to rebuild
on the way, and this engine rebuilds nothing.

A Jedi Academy bot now plays a retail multiplayer level with no import refused
anywhere, sabre loaded and the bolt branch live. Jedi Outcast does the same out
of the same shared store.

### The step that was nearly missed

A vertex is modelled where it belongs in the whole model, not relative to the
bone that carries it. So skinning one means undoing where that bone rests
before applying where it is now, and with nothing animated the two cancel and
the vertex stays exactly where it was modelled.

Leaving that out is invisible: the arithmetic runs, the matrix comes out
orthonormal, and every number looks like a number. Against a retail model it
put the right hand's tag **26.7 units** from the hand. With the undo in place
it lands **0.644 units** from `rhang_tag_bone`, which is where a tag on a hand
belongs. Nothing in the unit tests would have caught it, because a fixture
whose rest matrices are the identity cannot tell the two apart. It was caught
by checking against a real model, which is the only thing that could have.

A second bug went the same way: the files this store now keeps were held in a
vector, so keeping the second one moved the first and left every model holding
a dangling pointer to its own mesh. They are held one at a time now.

## Honest notes on this measurement

Five things here were got wrong first and corrected, and the corrections are
the interesting part.

The deviation reading was rejected once on a bad test -- counting exact zeros
in a hand-animated pool, where they are rare for reasons that have nothing to
do with the encoding. A static prop settles it in one look.

The `1/64` scale was then rejected on a second bad test: comparing a bone's
displacement against the length of the bone, when the quantity is not a local
offset at all.

And Outcast's humanoid was read as evidence against the composition rule when
it was evidence about its own hierarchy. In all three the arithmetic was fine
and the thing being compared against was wrong, which is the failure worth
watching for in work of this kind.

The fourth is the worst, because it shipped. The rotation's mid-point and
divisor were fitted to the data and came out two parts off, and the data said
so plainly -- a unit quaternion whose length is 0.9998 is not a unit
quaternion -- and that residual was written off as noise in the animation
rather than chased. Reading the engine took a few minutes and settled it
exactly. The lesson is not that fitting is wrong; it is that a fit which
leaves a systematic residual has not finished.

And one negative here was reported too confidently before being softened: the
search that found no multiply by fourteen. The instruction exists, at
`0x497a23`. A sweep that desynchronises cannot support a negative, and saying
so afterwards is weaker than not saying it in the first place.

The fifth was caught by the only thing that could have caught it. Skinning
without undoing a bone's rest produces a matrix that is orthonormal, finite and
entirely wrong, and no unit test built on identity fixtures can tell the
difference -- the two readings agree exactly when the rest matrix is the
identity. It took running the thing against a retail model and asking whether
the right hand's tag was anywhere near the right hand. It was 26.7 units away.
The lesson is narrow and worth keeping: a fixture that makes a test pass for
the wrong reason is worse than no test, and geometry needs at least one check
against something real.
